# Scientific Dossier: *Umbrachoir lyticus*

**Common name:** Black Choir Virus  
**Ecosystem role:** Virus - Colony rhythm parasite

## Profile

A matte-black polyhedral particle surrounded by vibrating filaments. This cryptid shares the same deep-sea hydrothermal-vent ecosystem as *Hydrosaltator amictus*.

- **Habitat:** dark mineral films shared by vent colonies
- **Type:** Virus
- **Role:** Colony rhythm parasite

## Field behavior

Makes every infected colony click one note off-key, apparently for comedy.

## Detailed behavior

Black Choir Virus particles lodge in sheltered mineral films and infect compatible cells remaining in abandoned colonies. Water movement and contact distribute them; the particles themselves do not hunt or sing. Within an infected colony, altered cell activity disrupts the usual timing of local chemical and mechanical signals, creating the fictional off-key clicking described by observers.

As infected cells break apart, released material becomes food for Mycocladus and other recyclers. Sparse surviving hosts produce isolated disturbances, while dense films can lose their coordinated rhythm across a wider patch. Particles left without a host persist as inert structures until damaged or carried elsewhere. The shared Black Choir nickname does not make this virus the same organism as Thanatovibrio umbracaris.

## Detailed anatomy

**Polyhedral shell.** The matte-black particle has a faceted capsid with a reinforced edge network. Its dark exterior is a fictional surface characteristic of the shell. A protected central cavity contains the viral genome, with no cellular organelles or independent metabolic apparatus.

**Filament ring.** Fine filaments emerge around an equatorial belt, giving the particle its vibration-ring silhouette. Flexible bases allow passive movement in flowing water and contact with a film's surface. These structures aid attachment in the setting; they are not muscles, vocal cords, or an active propulsion system.

**Release pores.** Small gated sites interrupt the capsid at selected vertices. In the fictional anatomy, an attachment-triggered opening permits genome delivery into a compatible host cell. These particle-level openings are distinct from the ruptures through which an infected cell releases newly assembled viruses.

**Surface contacts and assembly.** Short binding tips at the filament ends retain the particle in sheltered film crevices. New capsids and filament belts are assembled using the infected cell's machinery, then loaded with genomes. Damaged or empty shells can remain caught in the mineral film after an outbreak has passed.
