# Scientific Dossier: *Mycocladus sulfuris*

**Common name:** Sulfur Lace  
**Ecosystem role:** Decomposer - Sulfur-film recycler

## Profile

A branching amber filament network with hooked recycling nodules. This cryptid shares the same deep-sea hydrothermal-vent ecosystem as *Hydrosaltator amictus*.

- **Habitat:** dead mineral mats and abandoned shell fragments
- **Type:** Decomposer
- **Role:** Sulfur-film recycler

## Detailed behavior

Sulfur Lace advances across abandoned shells and spent films by extending its branch tips into the richest patches. Hooked nodules hold scraps in place while secreted digestive compounds loosen the organic residue. Dissolved products are absorbed locally or released into the matrix, where Saccharoplasma can use the carbon-rich fraction.

Branches slow when they meet living, well-maintained housing and flourish on discarded material. As a patch is exhausted, older inner strands collapse while the outer lace continues spreading. Broken tips can establish new patches if they settle on suitable debris. Strong flow makes the network hug the substrate and retain only firmly caught scraps.

## Detailed anatomy

**Branching framework.** Amber filaments form a flattened mesh rather than a single stalk. Flexible walls surround narrow living channels, and internal partitions divide the mesh into compartments. Small communicating openings allow materials to move along intact strands; a damaged compartment can be sealed without sacrificing every connected branch.

**Recycling nodules.** Hooked swellings occur at branch junctions and mature tips. Each has a curved gripping surface, a sheltered digestion pocket, and absorptive lining. The hook holds a fragment against the pocket while enzymes act, limiting the loss of useful products to passing water.

**Sulfur film.** A thin mineral-stained coating gives the lace its amber color. It traps fine particles and provides a working surface for decomposition. The coating is renewed as older deposits crack or become clogged; it is distinct from the living filament wall underneath.

**Growth and reserves.** Rounded storage bodies cluster behind active tips, supplying extension when food arrives unevenly. Basal adhesive threads secure the older network to shell or rock. Mature branch ends can thicken into compact resting fragments, retaining a small reserve until fresh residue permits growth.

## Relationships in the wider food web

Living growth tips are also prey for *Palpophaga pilosa*. Grazed branches seal the damaged compartment and redirect extension to neighboring tips; repeated cropping reduces the amount of recycled carbon released to Sugar-Silk.
